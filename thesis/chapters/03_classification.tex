\chapter{Classification approaches}
\label{cap:classification_approaches}
The following chapter surveys existing approaches to speech and music classification and presents the models selected as references for this work, including their published trade-offs, the adjustments made to fit the 3-class on-line setting, and an analysis of their streaming behavior.

\section{Overview}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/general_classifier.pdf}
\caption{Example diagram of a generic speech/music classifier.}
\label{fig:general_classifier}
\end{figure}

Classifiers are algorithms whose goal is to assign an input sample to one of a set of predefined classes.
A minimal processing pipeline of a generic classification system is shown in \Cref{fig:general_classifier}.
Its first stage consists of pre-processing steps whose details depend on the architecture and application of the specific classifier.
For on-line audio classification, this stage buffers and segments the input signal under the constraints described in \Cref{subsec:constraints}.

Raw input samples do not provide a robust representation of an audio signal, which motivates the use of feature extraction in the majority of audio classification pipelines.
These pipelines are themselves typically embedded as pre-processing modules in larger systems, where the classifier prevents subsequent stages from operating on unsuitable input.

Published speech and music classifiers fall into three broad lines: hand-engineered acoustic features paired with a traditional learner, learned features paired with a linear classifier, and end-to-end neural models.
The three implemented references in this work cover the first and the third of these lines, and the remainder of this section sketches the wider landscape they sit in.

The hand-engineered feature line begins with the radio-monitoring discriminators of Saunders~\cite{saunders1996realtime} and Scheirer and Slaney~\cite{scheirer1997discriminator}, who combined statistical measures of zero-crossing rate, spectral centroid, spectral flux, and modulation energy with simple Gaussian classifiers.
Lavner and Ruinskiy~\cite{lavner2009dt} produced a decision-tree-based system with a hand-engineered three-stage rule-based sieve over time- and frequency-domain statistics.
Khonglah and Prasanna~\cite{khonglah2016gmmsvm} pushed this line further with speech-specific features (excitation source, vocal tract, modulation spectrum) and a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) or Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier.
Bhattacharjee, Prasanna, and Guha~\cite{bhattacharjee2018sps} treated spectral peak locations across frames as time series and computed periodicity, zero-crossing rate, and second-order statistics on these sequences with a support-vector classifier.

The learned-representation line begins with Hirvonen~\cite{hirvonen2014short}, who learned a sparse-coding dictionary from log-mel spectrograms and fed the encoded segments to a linear support-vector classifier.
End-to-end neural models replaced the dictionary with a learned convolutional or recurrent backbone, and Lemaire and Holzapfel~\cite{lemaire2019tcn} showed that a causal Temporal Convolutional Network outperforms recurrent references on broadcast audio while remaining feed-forward and parallelizable.
The published trade-offs of the three implemented references are revisited alongside their adaptations in \Cref{sec:traditional_methods,sec:neural_methods}.

\section{Output classes}
\label{sec:output_classes}

Every classifier in this thesis assigns each frame to one of three classes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speech}: human speech, even with background music or other speakers.
    \item \textbf{Music}: instrumental, vocal (including a cappella), or unconventional music content.
    \item \textbf{Background}: non-speech, non-music audio, including silence, ambient sound, and noise.
\end{itemize}
This formulation departs from the 2-class formulation of the reference papers~\cite{lavner2009dt,khonglah2016gmmsvm,lemaire2019tcn} (introduced in the following sections).
The other mentioned but non-implemented papers also do not address it~\cite{bhattacharjee2018sps,hirvonen2014short}.

The third class makes the classifier deployable on its own.
Codecs see silence and ambient noise alongside speech and music. A speech-vs-music-only model has no defined behavior on those inputs, so a codec deployment would otherwise need a separate activity gate to filter them out before classification.
Folding the \emph{Background} class into the classifier itself absorbs that decision, removing the need for a separate activity gate.

Which audio signals fall into each class is detailed in \Cref{sec:dataset_overview}.

\section{Traditional methods}
\label{sec:traditional_methods}
Traditional methods of speech and music classification refer to the use of machine learning algorithms such as GMM, a decision tree, or SVM.
Compared to deep learning, they offer low computational complexity at inference and rely on a hand-engineered feature set.
Feature selection is required to keep the feature space compact while preserving its discriminative power.
In practice this is done either through exhaustive experimentation or with automated selection algorithms, ranging from filter methods such as the analysis-of-variance (ANOVA) F-test~\cite{guyon2003featsel} to wrapper methods such as Sequential Floating Forward Selection~\cite{pudil1994floating}.

The following models were implemented as reference non-neural solutions for the speech and music classification problem.
All traditional methods are implemented with the Python library \texttt{scikit-learn}~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}.
Feature extraction is implemented using NumPy~\cite{harris2020numpy}, SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}, or librosa~\cite{mcfee2015librosa} primitives.

All three models were originally designed as binary classifiers and were adapted to the 3-class task.

\subsection{Decision tree}

\paragraph{Original method.}
Lavner and Ruinskiy~\cite{lavner2009dt} proposed a hand-engineered three-stage sieve over a pool of nineteen time- and frequency-domain features.
The features are computed on 40\,ms frames with 50\% overlap and aggregated per feature across 4\,s segments at 100\,ms hop via mean, standard deviation, and the mean and standard deviation of the frame-by-frame difference.

On the Scheirer-Slaney dataset~\cite{scheirer1997discriminator} the authors report $99.07\,\%$ accuracy at low compute cost suitable for real-time operation, evaluated on a much larger test dataset than most prior work.
Reported limitations are accuracy drops to roughly $93\,\%$ on electronic and pop music and a transition delay of approximately $6$\,s that follows from the 4\,s segment window.

The features used in the original work are: Short-Time Energy, Zero-Crossing Rate, Band Energy Ratio, Autocorrelation Coefficient, 10 Mel-Frequency Cepstrum Coefficients (MFCCs), the Euclidean norm of the MFCC difference vector, Spectral Rolloff Point, Spectral Centroid, Spectral Flux, and Spectral Spread.

A non-parametric class-conditional probability density function is estimated for every feature, and five thresholds are derived per feature: two extreme thresholds (zero training-error per class), two high-probability thresholds, and one separation threshold.
A separate feature subset is selected for each threshold type, using inclusion-fraction ranking, separation-power scoring, and floating selection over a scatter-matrix criterion.

At inference, each 4\,s segment receives a grade in $[-1, +1]$ from how many of its features surpass each threshold.
The grade is then smoothed by an exponentially-decaying weighted average of past segment decisions and discretized against an adaptive boundary.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
I replaced the sieve with a single scikit-learn \texttt{DecisionTreeClassifier}, preceded by a \texttt{StandardScaler} and an ANOVA F-test \texttt{SelectKBest} that picks the ten most discriminative components in a single step instead of running the paper's per-threshold multi-stage scheme, simplifying the implementation and aiding extensibility.

\paragraph{On-line adaptation.}
To account for the on-line constraint of the task at hand, I reduced the frames from 40\,ms to 20\,ms, preserving the 50\% overlap, and replaced the 4\,s segments with a 300\,ms rolling buffer of features over which the statistical aggregates are computed.

I kept the paper's exponentially-decaying decision smoothing (forgetting factor of 0.9 over a 30-deep deque of post-classifier probabilities) but operate it per frame at 10\,ms intervals.
I dropped the adaptive boundary and the paper's four-level output mode, since the learned tree predicts directly via $\arg\max$ over its class probabilities.
These changes let the classifier predict each frame's class every 10\,ms, comfortably satisfying the on-line constraint.

\paragraph{Three-class extension.}
The paper trains only on speech and music, explicitly excluding silent and noisy frames.
For the 3-class task, I gave background frames their own training labels and let the tree fit all three classes simultaneously.
The smoothed $\arg\max$ extends naturally over $\{$speech, music, background$\}$ without further changes.

\subsection{Gaussian mixture model and support vector machine}

\paragraph{Original method.}
Khonglah and Prasanna~\cite{khonglah2016gmmsvm} proposed a Gaussian mixture model and a support vector machine sharing the same feature extraction pipeline.
The authors report the SVM reaching $95.87\,\%$ classification accuracy on the Scheirer-Slaney dataset~\cite{scheirer1997discriminator}, and the speech-specific features generalize across the Scheirer-Slaney, GTZAN~\cite{tzanetakis2002gtzan}, and Indian Broadcast News datasets with low mutual correlation between them.
Reported limitations are the threshold variant's reliance on hand-tuning and the work's restriction to non-vocal music.

\paragraph{Features.}
The original work specifies 30\,ms frames with 1\,ms hop for speech-specific features.
Frame size for the existing features is not specified.

Speech-specific features: Normalized Autocorrelation Peak Strength (NAPS) of the Zero Frequency Filtered Signal, Peak-to-Sidelobe Ratio (PSR) of the Hilbert Envelope of Linear Prediction (LP) Residual, Log-Mel Spectrum Energy, and Modulation-Spectrum Energy at 4\,Hz.
Existing features: Zero-Crossing Rate, Spectral Centroid, Spectral Flux, and Spectral Rolloff Point.

The extracted features are then accumulated over 1\,s non-overlapping windows.
Long-term statistics are computed and passed to the classifiers: the mean and variance, plus the percentage of low-energy frames (frames whose total energy is below one-third of the average energy in the window).

\paragraph{On-line adaptation.}
To run the feature-extraction pipeline on-line, I kept the original 30\,ms frame length and 1\,ms hop for NAPS and log-mel, and vectorized their computation.
Since the Levinson-Durbin LP analysis~\cite{makhoul1975lp} is sequential by nature, I sped up its calculation by reducing its cadence to 100\,Hz.
A hop-length scan confirmed that PSR remains $>0.99$ correlated with the 1\,ms reference up to a 10\,ms hop.

For the existing features, for which the paper does not specify a hop, I use 30\,ms frames at 15\,ms hop to match the streaming output rate.
Finally, I replaced the 1\,s non-overlapping statistics window with a rolling buffer updated every 15\,ms.

Khonglah and Prasanna's work does not specify any feature normalization.
I added it to both classifiers for reasons described in the following subsections.

\subsubsection{Gaussian mixture model}

The reference GMM uses two models for the speech and music classes.
I added a third model to accommodate the \emph{Background} class.
Each of the models has eight mixture components with diagonal covariance matrices.

The GMM's sensitivity to outliers motivates feature normalization using \texttt{RobustScaler}, which scales by their per-feature median and interquartile range.
I then clamp the scaled features to $\pm10$ to prevent residual outliers from triggering the kind of numerical-instability failures known to affect GMM fitting~\cite[§9.2.2]{bishop2006prml}.
Without the clamp, training produced \texttt{NaN} values and aborted.

Frames were originally classified by the sigmoid-mapped log-likelihood delta against a learned threshold.
At the 15\,ms frame hop, per-frame predictions flicker between classes because each frame's feature aggregates are computed over slightly different rolling windows.

Averaging the classifier outputs over a short buffer damps this flicker.
Concretely, I average the per-class log-likelihoods over a 66-frame rolling buffer ($\approx 1$\,s at 15\,ms hop).
I replaced sigmoid mapping with $\arg\max$ over the smoothed log-likelihood vector.

\subsubsection{Support vector machine}

The reference describes SVM with regularization parameter $C = 1$ and radial basis function (RBF) kernel with $\gamma = 3$.
I kept these hyperparameters identical and implemented the SVM using scikit-learn's \texttt{SVC}.
No adaptation from 2 to 3 classes was needed, because \texttt{SVC} supports multiclass classification by default.

The RBF kernel is dominated by pairwise Euclidean distances between feature vectors, so feature magnitudes need to be on the same scale.
A plain \texttt{StandardScaler} (z-score normalization~\cite{han2012preprocessing}) is sufficient here.

The reference trained the SVM on 160 fifteen-second recordings, yielding $2{,}400$ 1\,s non-overlapping windows, and produced a binary classification per window.
The RBF kernel generally scales between $O(n^2)$ and $O(n^3)$ with training set size.

Training on the full training set, which produces over 20 million 15\,ms frames, is infeasible.
To make training manageable, I reduced the training set to $50{,}000$ frames per class.
This subset of $150{,}000$ frames is still roughly $60\times$ bigger than the training set used in Khonglah and Prasanna's work.

The reference SVM produced one decision per non-overlapping 1\,s window with no further smoothing.
I average the 3-element decision-function vectors over a 20-frame rolling buffer ($\approx 300$\,ms) to stabilize the per-frame decisions at the 15\,ms streaming hop.
Compared to the GMM, the SVM smoothing buffer is shorter (20 vs 66 frames) because SVM's outputs are more stable and in a comparable range across frames.
Finally, the class of the frame is determined by $\arg\max$ over the smoothed vector.

\section{Deep learning methods}
\label{sec:neural_methods}
Deep learning models eliminate the hand-engineered feature step that constrains the traditional classifiers in \Cref{sec:traditional_methods}.
Given a normalized log-mel spectrogram, the network learns its own internal representations and the decision boundary jointly, trading the interpretability of explicit features for capacity to capture patterns that hand-engineered statistics can miss.

Several families of neural architectures have been applied to audio classification.
The earliest reference is the multi-layer perceptron~\cite{rumelhart1986backprop}, which processes each input frame independently and serves as the reference against which sequence-aware models are compared.
The Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)~\cite{lecun1998cnn}, originally designed for image recognition, has been applied to spectrograms by treating them as two-dimensional images and learning local time-frequency patterns through stacked convolutions, producing strong results on tasks where the input window is fixed.
The Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)~\cite{elman1990rnn} models temporal dependencies explicitly by carrying a hidden state forward through time, and the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) variant~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} solves the vanishing-gradient problem that limits vanilla RNNs.

\subsection{Convolutional neural network}
The CNN's central operation is the convolution: a learned kernel slides across the input, computing a dot product at each position to produce a feature map.
For a one-dimensional input $x$ and a learned kernel $w$ of size $K$, the convolution at position $t$ is
\begin{align}
\label{eq:conv_1d}
y[t] &= \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w[k] \cdot x[t-k]
\end{align}
the same expression extends to two dimensions by indexing over both the time and frequency axes of the spectrogram.

In practice, a convolutional layer learns many kernels in parallel.
Each is called a \textbf{filter}, and the feature map it produces is one \textbf{channel} of the layer's output.
A conv layer is therefore characterized by two hyperparameters: the \textbf{kernel size} $K$ (the spatial extent of each filter) and the \textbf{filter count} $F$ (the number of output channels).

Two properties make this attractive for spectrogram inputs.

First, the kernel weights are shared across all positions, so the same time-frequency pattern is detected regardless of where it appears in the input.

Second, stacking convolutional layers builds a hierarchy: early layers respond to simple local patterns, and deeper layers compose them into longer-range structures.
The set of input positions that influence a given output is called the network's \textbf{receptive field}.
It grows linearly with depth, by $K-1$ positions per added layer.

A special case is the $1{\times}1$ convolution ($K = 1$), which acts as a learned linear combination of the input channels at each frame.
It does not enlarge the receptive field but only mixes or re-projects channels.

\subsection{Temporal convolutional network}
Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) were introduced by Bai et al.~\cite{bai2018tcn} as a feed-forward alternative to recurrent sequence models.
A TCN keeps the CNN's parameter sharing and feature hierarchy but applies them along the time axis only, with one modification that adapts the architecture to long sequences: dilation.

\textbf{Dilated} convolutions read input samples at stride $d$ instead of at consecutive positions, so the same $K$ kernel weights (with $K$ odd) span $(K-1)\cdot d + 1$ input positions centered on $t$:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:dilated_conv}
y[t] &= \sum_{k=-(K-1)/2}^{(K-1)/2} w[k] \cdot x[t - k \cdot d]
\end{align}
Stacking $L$ such convolutions at distinct dilations $d_1, \ldots, d_L$ yields a receptive field of
\begin{align}
\label{eq:tcn_rf_general}
\mathrm{RF} &= 1 + (K-1) \sum_{l=1}^{L} d_l.
\end{align}
With exponentially-increasing dilation $d_l = 2^{l-1}$, this simplifies to
\begin{align}
\label{eq:tcn_rf_geom}
\mathrm{RF} &= 1 + (K-1)(2^L - 1),
\end{align}
exponential in $L$.

Dilation lets the network access long-range temporal context without the rolling feature buffer or statistical aggregates that capture context over multi-frame windows.

Lemaire and Holzapfel~\cite{lemaire2019tcn} demonstrated this architecture on broadcast speech and music detection, where their non-causal extension reportedly reached F1 scores (explained in \Cref{subsec:exp_eval_metrics}) of $0.971$ on music and $0.957$ on speech, outperformed their picked references, and trained roughly $4\times$ faster.
The non-causal kernel violates the on-line constraint defined in \Cref{subsec:constraints} because it reads future input, and its receptive field grows much larger than the training chunk length.
The TCN's feed-forward design also makes it parallelizable to train and bounded in memory at inference.

\subsection{Causal TCN}
\label{subsec:causal_tcn}
The on-line constraint defined in \Cref{subsec:constraints} requires that each output frame depend only on past inputs.
The causal TCN, presented in the same work by Lemaire and Holzapfel, enforces this by shifting the kernel of \Cref{eq:dilated_conv} so it covers only past positions.
The index $k$ runs from $0$ to $K-1$ instead of being centered on zero, giving the causal dilated convolution
\begin{align}
\label{eq:causal_dilated_conv}
y[t] &= \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w[k] \cdot x[t - k \cdot d]
\end{align}
where every sampled input $x[t - k \cdot d]$ lies at or before $t$.

\caption{Causal dilated convolutions.}
\label{fig:tcn_dilation}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:tcn_dilation} shows three such convolutions with kernel size $2$ and dilations $1, 2, 4$.
The rightmost output neuron's receptive field spans all $8$ past input positions.

This causal variant of the TCN is adopted as the reference architecture for this work.
All subsequent mentions of \textbf{TCN} refer to this causal variant.

\subsubsection{Architecture}

The TCN architecture builds from three nested levels: the residual block, the stack, and the backbone.

A \textbf{residual block} (\Cref{fig:tcn_residual_block}) is the smallest unit.
Let $c$ denote the number of convolutions per dilation level inside a block.
A block contains $c$ causal dilated convolutions that share the same dilation factor $d$ and kernel size $K$, each followed by weight normalization, a non-linearity, and dropout.
A skip connection adds the block's input to the output of the second dropout, with a $1{\times}1$ convolution inserted on the residual path when the input and output channel counts differ.
Bai et al.~\cite{bai2018tcn}, Lemaire and Holzapfel~\cite{lemaire2019tcn} (via the keras-tcn~\cite{remy2018kerastcn} implementation), and this work all set $c = 2$ and use ReLU as the non-linearity.
A block thus replaces a single convolution as the per-dilation unit, so $L$ from \Cref{eq:tcn_rf_geom} now counts blocks.


\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/tcn_residual_block.pdf}
\caption{TCN residual block.}
\label{fig:tcn_residual_block}
\end{figure}

Each convolution extends the receptive field back by $(K-1) \cdot d$ frames, so $c$ such convolutions together extend it by $c(K-1) \cdot d$.
\Cref{eq:tcn_rf_geom} therefore generalizes to
\begin{align}
\label{eq:tcn_rf_geom_c}
\mathrm{RF} &= 1 + c(K-1)(2^L - 1).
\end{align}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/tcn_rf_blocks.pdf}
\caption{Receptive field with two convolutions per block.}
\label{fig:tcn_rf_blocks}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:tcn_rf_blocks} shows the same kernel size and dilations as \Cref{fig:tcn_dilation} ($K = 2$, dilations $1, 2, 4$) but with $c = 2$.

A \textbf{stack} groups $L$ residual blocks with dilations $1, 2, 4, \ldots, 2^{L-1}$.
Stacking blocks with doubling dilation, rather than letting the dilation grow indefinitely, keeps the maximum dilation bounded while still multiplying the receptive field.

The \textbf{backbone} (\Cref{fig:tcn_backbone}) chains $S$ stacks together.
The input log-mel spectrogram is first projected by a $1{\times}1$ convolution to a feature map whose channel count matches the residual blocks' filter count.
A final $1{\times}1$ convolution at the end of the backbone maps the feature map to the three output classes, producing the per-frame logit tensor.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/tcn_backbone.pdf}
\caption{TCN backbone. $F$ represents the filter count in each block.}
\label{fig:tcn_backbone}
\end{figure}

Across $S$ stacks, \Cref{eq:tcn_rf_geom_c} extends to
\begin{align}
\label{eq:tcn_rf_full}
\mathrm{RF} = 1 + c S (K-1)(2^L - 1).
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Implementation details and adaptations}
\label{subsubsec:tcn_adjustments}

The reference network operates on log-mel spectrograms.
The audio is resampled to 22\,050\,Hz mono, a Short-Time Fourier Transform is computed with a Hann window of 1024 samples and a hop of 512 samples, and the squared magnitude is passed through 80 mel filters spaced between 27.5\,Hz and 8\,kHz.
The resulting features are put on a logarithmic scale and normalized to zero mean and unit variance over the training set.
Reference training uses fixed-length chunks of 270 frames at batch size 32, optimizes binary cross-entropy with stochastic gradient descent (SGD, momentum $m = 0.9$), divides the learning rate by ten when the validation loss has not improved for three epochs, and stops once five consecutive epochs show no improvement.

The hyperparameters adopted in this work fall within the ranges the paper allows.
Both are listed in \Cref{tab:tcn_paper_search}.

The network has been implemented using the PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} library.
The paper's recipe needs two adaptations for this setup.

First, the output layer projects to three classes (speech, music, and background) instead of two, producing a $(B, 3, T)$ logit tensor at the same frame rate as the input spectrogram.
Here $B$ is the batch size (32 during training, 1 during streaming inference) and $T$ is the number of frames per forward pass (128 during training).
Multi-label compatibility has been kept through per-class Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE).
This way, no semantic change is needed to adapt the output from 2 to 3 classes.
Implementation-wise, \texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss} has been used, which is numerically equivalent to BCE + sigmoid but applies the sigmoid in log-space for numerical stability.
The final prediction is picked from the per-class probability vector using $\arg\max$.

Second, the chunk length is reduced from 270 frames to 128.
At the original 270 frames ($\approx 6.3$\,s), 61\,\% of the speech clips in the training set (described in \Cref{cap:dataset}) were too short to produce a single chunk, which biased the model toward music.
128 frames ($\approx 3$\,s) recovers the full distribution.

\section{Streaming inference}
\label{sec:streaming_inference}

The on-line constraint defined in \Cref{subsec:constraints} requires every classifier to produce a fresh prediction at the streaming output rate using only past audio.

The traditional models in \Cref{sec:traditional_methods} satisfy this by construction (their feature extractor is per-frame and the smoothing buffers accumulate decisions on the fly), so no further analysis is needed at the architectural level.

The TCN, in contrast, is a pure batch function from a mel chunk to per-frame logits, so its on-line behavior depends on the architecture itself.
Causality of the dilated convolutions guarantees that the streaming output is bit-identical to the corresponding off-line forward pass on the same audio, with no padding, look-ahead, or boundary correction.
This bit-identicality is verified end-to-end by a parity check that feeds one second of fixed-seed random audio through both forward paths and requires the two resulting output tensors to match exactly, element-wise.

Both model families therefore meet the on-line constraint at the architectural level.
Per-frame latency, real-time factor, and end-to-end frame timing are reported in \Cref{subsec:exp_complexity_results}.
