\chapter{Classification Approaches}
\label{cap:classification_approaches}
The following chapter describes the general architecture of existing classifiers, presents models selected as references for the proposed solution, and adjustments made to these models to make them fit the task of 3-class on-line speech and music classification.

\section{Overview}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/general_classifier.pdf}
\label{fig:general_classifier}
\caption{Example diagram of generic speech/music classifier}
\end{figure}

Classifiers are algorithms whose goal is to assign an input sample to one of a set of predefined classes.

Figure~\ref{fig:general_classifier} illustrates a minimal processing pipeline of a generic classification system.

The first stage of the pipeline consists of pre-processing steps, which are largely influenced by the architecture of the specific classifier and its application.
In the case of on-line audio classification, buffering and segmentation of the input signal are required due to the constraints described in \cref{subsec:constraints}.

Raw input samples do not provide a robust representation of an audio signal, which motivates the use of feature extraction in the majority of audio classification pipelines.

Although a speech and music classifier can operate as a standalone application, audio classifiers are most commonly used as pre-processing components in larger processing pipelines.
As a module in this context, classifiers often serve to prevent subsequent processing stages from wasting computational resources or from operating on inactive or invalid input.

\section{Traditional Methods}
\label{sec:traditional_methods}
Traditional methods of speech and music classification refer to the use of machine learning algorithms such as Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), Decision Tree (DT), or Support Vector Machine (SVM).

The advantage of using machine learning models in comparison to deep learning algorithms is the low computational complexity in both training and inference stages.

A key drawback of traditional methods is their reliance on a hand-engineered feature set.
Feature selection is required to keep the feature space compact while preserving its discriminative power.
In practice this is done either through exhaustive experimentation or with automated selection algorithms, ranging from filter methods such as the ANOVA F-test~\cite{BATHKE2004413} to wrapper methods such as Sequential Floating Forward Selection~\cite{PUDIL19941119}.

The following models were re-implemented as reference non-neural solutions for the speech and music classification problem.

All core implementations mentioned and \texttt{highlighted} in this section come from the Python library \texttt{scikit-learn}~\cite{pedregosa2018scikitlearnmachinelearningpython}.
Feature extraction is implemented using NumPy~\cite{2020NumPy-Array}, SciPy~\cite{2020SciPy-NMeth}, or librosa~\cite{McFee2015librosaAA} primitives.

All three models were originally designed as binary classifiers and were adapted to the 3-class task.
The implementation details and differences are described in the following subsections. 

\subsection{Decision Tree}
Lavner and Ruinskiy~\cite{Lavner2009-zo} proposed a hand-crafted three-stage sieve over a pool of nineteen time- and frequency-domain features, computed on 40\,ms frames with 50\% overlap and aggregated per feature across 4\,s segments at 100\,ms hop via mean, standard deviation, and the mean and standard deviation of the frame-by-frame difference.

The features used in the original work are: Short-Time Energy, Zero-Crossing Rate, Band Energy Ratio, Autocorrelation Coefficient, 10 Mel-Frequency Cepstrum Coefficients (MFCCs), the Euclidean norm of the MFCC difference vector, Spectral Rolloff Point, Spectral Centroid, Spectral Flux, and Spectral Spread.

A non-parametric class-conditional probability density function (PDF) is estimated for every feature, and five thresholds are derived per feature: two extreme thresholds (zero training-error per class), two high-probability thresholds, and one separation threshold.
A separate feature subset is selected for each threshold type, using inclusion-fraction ranking, separation-power scoring, and floating selection over a scatter-matrix criterion.
At inference, each 4\,s segment receives a grade in $[-1, +1]$ from how many of its features surpass each threshold; the grade is then smoothed by an exponentially-decaying weighted average of past segment decisions and discretized against an adaptive boundary.

In this work the sieve is replaced by a single scikit-learn \texttt{DecisionTreeClassifier}, preceded by a \texttt{StandardScaler} and an ANOVA F-test \texttt{SelectKBest} that picks the ten most discriminative components in a single step instead of running the paper's per-threshold multi-stage scheme, simplifying the implementation and aiding extensibility.

To account for the on-line constraint of the task at hand, the frames were reduced from 40\,ms to 20\,ms, preserving the 50\% overlap, and the 4\,s segments were replaced by a 300\,ms rolling buffer of features over which the statistical aggregates are computed.
Decision smoothing keeps the paper's exponentially-decaying form (forgetting factor of 0.9 over a 30-deep deque of post-classifier probabilities) but operates per frame at 10\,ms intervals. 
The adaptive boundary and the paper's four-level output mode are dropped, since the learned tree commits directly via $\arg\max$ over its class probabilities.
These changes let the classifier predict each frame's class every 10\,ms, comfortably satisfying the on-line constraint.

The paper trains only on speech and music, explicitly excluding silent and noisy frames.
For the 3-class task, inactive frames carry their own training labels and the tree fits all three classes simultaneously; the smoothed $\arg\max$ extends naturally over $\{$speech, music, inactive$\}$ without further changes.

\subsection{Gaussian Mixture Model and Support Vector Machine}
Khonglah and Prasanna~\cite{KHONGLAH201671} proposed a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) and a support vector machine (SVM) sharing the same feature extraction pipeline.

The original work specifies 30\,ms frames with 1\,ms hop for speech-specific features.
Frame size for the existing features is not specified.
Speech-specific features: Normalized Autocorrelation Peak Strength (NAPS) of the Zero Frequency Filtered Signal, Peak-to-Sidelobe Ratio (PSR) of the Hilbert Envelope of Linear Prediction (LP) Residual, Log-Mel Spectrum Energy, and Modulation-Spectrum Energy at 4\,Hz.
Existing features: Zero-Crossing Rate, Spectral Centroid, Spectral Flux, and Spectral Rolloff Point.
The extracted features are then accumulated over 1\,s non-overlapping windows.
Long-term statistics --- the mean and variance, plus the percentage of low-energy frames (frames whose total energy is below one-third of the average energy in the window) --- are computed and passed to the classifiers.

To run the feature-extraction pipeline online, we have kept the original 30\,ms frame size and 1\,ms shift for NAPS and log-mel, and vectorized their computation.
Since the Levinson-Durbin LP analysis~\cite{1451722} is sequential by nature, we have sped up its calculation by reducing its cadence to 100\,Hz.
A shift sweep confirmed that PSR remains $>0.99$ correlated with the 1\,ms reference up to a 10\,ms hop.

The existing features, for which the paper does not specify a hop, are computed on 30\,ms frames at 15\,ms hop to match the streaming output rate.
Finally, the 1\,s non-overlapping statistics window is replaced by a rolling buffer updated every 15\,ms.

Khonglah and Prasanna's work does not specify any feature normalization; we have added it to both classifiers for reasons described in the following subsections.

\subsubsection{Gaussian Mixture Model}
The reference GMM used two models for the speech and music classes.
We have added a third model to accommodate the inactive class.
Each of the models has eight mixture components with diagonal covariance matrices.

The GMM's sensitivity to outliers motivated us to normalize the features using \texttt{RobustScaler}, which scales by their per-feature median and interquartile range.
Scaled features are then clamped to $\pm10$ to prevent residual outliers from triggering the kind of numerical-instability failures known to affect GMM fitting~\cite[§9.2.2]{bishop2006prml}.
Without the clamp, training produced \texttt{NaN} values and aborted.

Frames were originally classified by the sigmoid-mapped log-likelihood delta against a learned threshold.
At the 15\,ms frame hop, per-frame predictions flicker between classes because each frame's feature aggregates are computed over slightly different rolling windows.
Averaging the classifier outputs over a short buffer damps this flicker.
Concretely, we average the per-class log-likelihoods over a 66-frame rolling buffer ($\approx 1$\,s at 15\,ms hop).
Sigmoid mapping was replaced by $\arg\max$ over the smoothed log-likelihood vector.

\subsubsection{Support Vector Machine}
The reference describes SVM with regularization parameter $C = 1$ and RBF kernel with $\gamma = 3$.
We have kept these hyperparameters identical and implemented the SVM using scikit-learn's \texttt{SVC}.
No adaptation from 2 to 3 classes was needed, because \texttt{SVC} supports multiclass classification by default.

The RBF kernel is dominated by pairwise Euclidean distances between feature vectors, so feature magnitudes need to be on the same scale.
A plain \texttt{StandardScaler} (z-score normalization~\cite{HAN201283}) is sufficient here.

The reference trained the SVM on 160 fifteen-second recordings, yielding $2{,}400$ 1\,s non-overlapping windows, and produced a binary classification per window.
The RBF kernel generally scales between $O(n^2)$ and $O(n^3)$ with training set size.
Training on our full training set --- which produces over 20 million 15\,ms frames --- is infeasible.
To make training manageable, we have reduced the training set to $50{,}000$ frames per class.
This subset of $150{,}000$ frames is roughly $60\times$ bigger than the training set used in Khonglah and Prasanna's work.

The reference SVM produced one decision per non-overlapping 1\,s window with no further smoothing.
We average the 3-element decision-function vectors over a 20-frame rolling buffer ($\approx 300$\,ms) to stabilize the per-frame decisions at the 15\,ms streaming hop.
Compared to the GMM, the SVM smoothing buffer is shorter (20 vs 66 frames) because SVM's outputs are more stable and in a comparable range across frames.
Finally, the class of the frame is determined by $\arg\max$ over the smoothed vector.







\section{Deep Learning Methods}
Deep learning models eliminate the hand-engineered feature step that constrains the traditional classifiers in \cref{sec:traditional_methods}.
Given a normalized log-mel spectrogram, the network learns its own internal representations and the decision boundary jointly, trading the interpretability of explicit features for capacity to capture patterns that hand-crafted statistics can miss.

Several families of neural architectures have been applied to audio classification.
The simplest, the multi-layer perceptron~\cite{rumelhart1986learning}, processes each input frame independently as a fixed-length vector and ignores temporal structure entirely; it is the historical baseline against which sequence-aware models are compared.
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)~\cite{726791}, originally designed for image recognition, has been applied to spectrograms by treating them as two-dimensional images and learning local time-frequency patterns through stacked convolutions, producing strong results on tasks where the input window is fixed.
Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)~\cite{elman1990finding} models temporal dependencies explicitly by carrying a hidden state forward through time, and the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) variant~\cite{10.1162/neco.1997.9.8.1735} solves the vanishing-gradient problem that limits vanilla RNNs.

\subsection{Convolutional Neural Network}
The CNN's central operation is the convolution: a small bank of learned kernels slides across the input, computing a dot product at each position to produce a feature map.
For a one-dimensional input $x$ and a learned kernel $w$ of size $K$, the convolution at position $t$ is
\begin{align}
\label{eq:conv_1d}
y[t] &= \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w[k] \cdot x[t-k]
\end{align}
the same expression extends to two dimensions by indexing over both the time and frequency axes of the spectrogram.

Two properties make this attractive for spectrogram inputs.

First, the kernel weights are shared across all positions, so the same time-frequency pattern is detected regardless of where it appears in the input.

Second, stacking convolutional layers builds a hierarchy: early layers respond to simple local patterns, and deeper layers compose them into longer-range structures.
The set of input positions that influence a given output --- the network's \textbf{receptive field} --- grows linearly with depth, by $K-1$ positions per added layer.

\subsection{Temporal Convolutional Network}
Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) were introduced by Bai et al.~\cite{bai2018empiricalevaluationgenericconvolutional} as a feed-forward alternative to recurrent sequence models.
A TCN keeps the CNN's parameter sharing and feature hierarchy but applies them along the time axis only, with one modification that adapts the architecture to long sequences: dilation.

\textbf{Dilated} convolutions read input samples at stride $d$ instead of at consecutive positions, so the same $K$ kernel weights (with $K$ odd) span $(K-1)\cdot d + 1$ input positions centered on $t$:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:dilated_conv}
y[t] &= \sum_{k=-(K-1)/2}^{(K-1)/2} w[k] \cdot x[t - k \cdot d]
\end{align}
Stacking $L$ such layers with dilation factors $d_1, \ldots, d_L$ yields a receptive field of
\begin{align}
\label{eq:tcn_rf_general}
\mathrm{RF} &= 1 + (K-1) \sum_{l=1}^{L} d_l.
\end{align}
With exponentially-increasing dilation $d_l = 2^{l-1}$, this simplifies to
\begin{align}
\label{eq:tcn_rf_geom}
\mathrm{RF} &= 1 + (K-1)(2^L - 1),
\end{align}
exponential in $L$, as illustrated in \cref{fig:tcn_dilation}.

Dilation lets the network access long-range temporal context without the rolling feature buffer or statistical aggregates that capture context over multi-frame windows.

Lemaire and Holzapfel~\cite{lemaire2019tcnspeechmusic} showed that a TCN built on this principle outperforms other architectures on broadcast speech-and-music detection while remaining parallelizable to train and bounded in memory at inference.

\subsection{Causal TCN}
The on-line constraint defined in \cref{subsec:constraints} requires that each output frame depend only on past inputs.
The causal TCN --- presented in the same work by Lemaire and Holzapfel --- enforces this by shifting the kernel of \cref{eq:dilated_conv} so it covers only past positions: $k$ runs from $0$ to $K-1$ instead of being centered on zero, giving the causal dilated convolution
\begin{align}
\label{eq:causal_dilated_conv}
y[t] &= \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w[k] \cdot x[t - k \cdot d]
\end{align}
where every sampled input $x[t - k \cdot d]$ lies at or before $t$.
We adopt this causal variant of the TCN as the reference architecture for this work.
All subsequent mentions of \textbf{TCN} refer to this causal variant.

\subsubsection{Implementation Details and Adaptations}

The reference network operates on log-mel spectrograms.
The audio is resampled to 22\,050\,Hz mono, a Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) is computed with a Hann window of 1024 samples and a hop of 512 samples, and the squared magnitude is passed through 80 mel filters spaced between 27.5\,Hz and 8\,kHz.
The resulting features are put on a logarithmic scale and normalized to zero mean and unit variance over the training set.
Reference training uses fixed-length chunks of 270 frames at batch size 32, optimizes binary cross-entropy with stochastic gradient descent (momentum $m = 0.9$), divides the learning rate by ten when the validation loss has not improved for three epochs, and stops once five consecutive epochs show no improvement.

The hyperparameters adopted in this work fall within the ranges the paper allows.
Both are listed in \cref{tab:tcn_paper_search}.

We have re-implemented the network using the \texttt{PyTorch}~\cite{paszke2019pytorchimperativestylehighperformance} library.
The paper's recipe needs two adaptations for our setup.

First, the output layer projects to three classes (speech, music, and inactive) instead of two, producing a $(B, 3, T)$ logit tensor at the same frame rate as the input spectrogram.
Here $B$ is the batch size (32 during training, 1 during streaming inference) and $T$ is the number of frames per forward pass (128 during training).
We have kept multi-label compatibility through per-class Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE).
This way, no semantic change is needed to adapt the output from 2 to 3 classes.
Implementation-wise we have used \texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss}, which is numerically equivalent to BCE + sigmoid but applies the sigmoid in log-space for numerical stability.
The final prediction is picked from the per-class probability vector using $\arg\max$.

Second, the chunk length is reduced from 270 frames to 128.
At the original 270 frames ($\approx 6.3$\,s), 61\,\% of the speech clips in our training set (described in \Cref{cap:dataset}) were too short to produce a single chunk, which biased the model toward music.
128 frames ($\approx 3$\,s) recovers the full distribution.

\section{Streaming Inference}
\label{sec:streaming_inference}

The on-line constraint defined in \cref{subsec:constraints} requires every classifier to produce a fresh prediction at the streaming output rate using only past audio.

The traditional models in \cref{sec:traditional_methods} satisfy this by construction --- their feature extractor is per-frame and the smoothing buffers accumulate decisions on the fly --- so no further analysis is needed at the architectural level.

The TCN, in contrast, is a pure batch function from a mel chunk to per-frame logits, so its on-line behavior depends on the architecture itself.
Causality of the dilated convolutions guarantees that the streaming output is bit-identical to the corresponding offline forward pass on the same audio, with no padding, look-ahead, or boundary correction.
The bit-identicality is verified by \texttt{scripts/parity\_tcn\_streaming.py}, which runs 1\,s of random audio through both paths and asserts \texttt{torch.equal} between the two probability tensors.
